The problem

One assistant, two incompatible requirements. Manuscript work is private and needs to run without a network; the heaviest reasoning needs a frontier model. Picking one means giving up the other.

What I built

An orchestrator that routes each request to the right place — a fast local model for private, offline, and quick work, Claude or GPT when the reasoning justifies the round trip. Underneath it, a memory layer pairing a vector store with a shared markdown story bible that holds characters, world rules, brand voice, and the social calendar, so every model in the system answers from the same canon.

Decisions worth naming

Manuscripts mirror from Google Drive into a local cache rather than being fetched live, which keeps latency and API calls out of the conversation loop. Edits dictated aloud apply to the live Google Doc as surgical in-place replacements through the Docs API, never whole-file overwrites, so formatting survives — and when a passage isn't found, or matches twice, the system reports it and changes nothing rather than guessing. Spoken observations are routed to a separate timestamped notes file and never written into the manuscript. The difference between an edit and a note is the kind of distinction that decides whether anyone trusts a system with their own work.

Voice

Wake word runs fully offline on a model I trained myself on "hey liam" rather than a stock keyword, in its own process because it has to listen system-wide. Speech in through whisper.cpp, out through Piper, interruptible at sentence boundaries so you can stop a reading mid-chapter and redirect it.

Testing

The test suite is not comprehensive and is not meant to be. It covers the three paths that have actually broken in real use — role-gated imports crashing a bare hub install, the mobile auth middleware, and chapter-number parsing — as a guard against regressing those specific incidents.

The problem

Readers working through a dense multi-book universe lose the thread — who a character was four hundred pages ago, when an event happened relative to another one, which world a place belongs to.

How it was built

Through agentic development rather than hand-written code: I scoped the work, reviewed what came back, iterated on the failures, and managed version control and releases throughout. That is a different skill from writing the codebase myself, and I describe it that way deliberately.

What shipping meant

Both store review processes, and the release cycles after them. Plenty of people have built something with AI assistance. Fewer have taken one through Apple's review and out the other side.

The problem

Generated imagery drifts. The same request on two different days produces two different looks, which is fine for one picture and fatal for a brand that has to stay recognizable across hundreds of them.

What I built

A written specification with a fixed field order — shot type, composition, primary subject, secondary elements, environment, key details, atmosphere, lighting, camera, style — plus modules added per scene type. Versioned and documented, so a result can be traced back to the spec that produced it.

Why it matters here

The useful part is not the prompts. It is that output became reproducible and auditable instead of a matter of luck and re-rolling, which is the same problem every organization deploying generative tooling runs into.

What it will do

Manuscript passage in, finished social unit out — image generated against the specification above, quote card composed, video assembled, caption drafted — with a human approval gate before anything publishes. Autonomous publishing is not the goal; a reviewable queue is.

What is being measured

Production time per unit and units shipped per week, recorded before any of it is automated. Without the baseline the result is an impression rather than a number, and an impression is not worth reporting.

Where this comes from

Ten years as a smart home installation technician across two tenures at Vivint, and somewhere north of seven thousand completed installations and service calls. Security panels, cameras, sensors, thermostats, locks, and every wireless mesh that refused to behave. Roughly twenty-seven jobs a week, sustained.

The part of that work most relevant here was never the wiring. It was standing in a living room explaining to somebody with no technical background why their lock was unreliable, in language that left them able to make a decision. Do that seven thousand times and you learn precisely how much explanation is useful and where it starts being for your own benefit rather than theirs.

Alongside it: four science fiction novels written, two published and a third close, and twenty-four and a half hours of audiobook audio engineered from raw recording to final master. The content pipeline — writing, audio, video, graphics — runs end to end through one person, down to the author website itself, hand-built in plain HTML and CSS, no framework.

The combination is unusual and it is the point. Most people building with AI have never installed the hardware it talks to. Most people who have installed the hardware are not building with AI.
